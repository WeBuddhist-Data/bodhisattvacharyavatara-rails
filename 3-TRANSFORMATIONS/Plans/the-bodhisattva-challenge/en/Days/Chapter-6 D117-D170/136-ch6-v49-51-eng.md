## Today's Verse

> I inflict harm on them,
> and they benefit me.
> Why then, perverse mind,
> do you become angry? ^6-49

> If I have good intentions,
> I will not go to hell.
> If I protect myself,
> what harm has come to them here? ^6-50

> But if I harm them in return,
> they are not protected,
> and my conduct is ruined.
> Thus, my ascetic practice will be destroyed. ^6-51

## 1) Introduction to Today's Practice

Today's practice is based on the forty-ninth, fiftieth, and fifty-first verses of the sixth chapter. We turn to our own unruly mind and ask a question. If we harm someone in return, will our patience break?

## 2) Commentary Explanation

The commentaries on these verses give us the following valuable explanation, which is easy to understand and encouraging. If we harm in return the person who harms us, the anger and wrongdoing in that person's mind grow even more. This also ruins the conduct of a bodhisattva, such as not giving up loving-kindness for all beings. Harming in return also ruins the four kinds of conduct of a monastic. When scolded, a monastic does not scold back. When someone is angry, a monastic is not angry back. When hit, a monastic does not hit back. When faults are exposed, a monastic does not expose faults back. For these reasons, the austerity of patience, which is the best conduct of a bodhisattva, will be ruined.

## 3) Today's Practice

**Actual Practice:** Today, even if others say bad words to me, I will not get angry in return.

**Explanation:** _(Ethics Practice)_ When others harm us and we do not harm back, our austerity is not ruined. This protects our good conduct from being ruined.
